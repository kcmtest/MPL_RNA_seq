Module 1 — RNA-seq Fundamentals

What does RNA-seq measure?

Biological signal

RNA-seq samples RNA molecules present in a biological specimen and converts them into sequenceable libraries.

RNA abundance → fragments → reads → mapped fragments → gene/transcript abundance

Important limitation

RNA-seq does not directly count intact RNA molecules.

Observed counts can be influenced by library preparation, GC content, sequencing depth, RNA quality and mapping ambiguity.

What can bulk RNA-seq tell us?

Expression

Which genes are expressed?

Sample relationships

Do replicates cluster together?

Differential expression

Which genes change between conditions?

Quality control

Are there outliers or batch effects?

Pathways

Which biological programs are enriched?

Phenotype

Which gene programs characterize the phenotype?

Common RNA-seq library strategies

Poly(A) selection

Enriches polyadenylated RNA and is commonly used for mRNA profiling.

rRNA depletion

Removes ribosomal RNA while retaining a broader range of RNA species.

Stranded libraries

Preserve transcript-strand information and improve assignment in overlapping loci.

Paired-end sequencing

Improves mapping across splice junctions and transcript structures.

Module 2 — Experimental Design

The most important analysis step happens before sequencing

A sophisticated statistical model cannot rescue a fundamentally confounded experiment.

  • What is the biological unit of replication?
  • What is the primary comparison?
  • Are replicates independent?
  • Are important covariates recorded?
  • Is batch balanced across conditions?
  • Is the design paired, longitudinal or factorial?

Biological vs technical replicates

Biological replicates

Independent biological samples that capture biological variability and support inference beyond the samples measured.

Technical replicates

Repeated measurement of the same biological material and mainly characterize technical variability.

Three sequencing lanes from the same RNA extraction are not three biological replicates.

Confounding example

Sample Condition Batch
S1 Control Batch1
S2 Control Batch1
S3 Treated Batch2
S4 Treated Batch2

Problem

Condition and batch are perfectly confounded. The model cannot determine whether the difference is caused by condition or batch.

Experimental design becomes a statistical model

Simple comparison

One primary factor:

Control vs Treated

Conceptually:

expression ~ condition

More complex experiment

Other factors may need to be represented:

batch • patient • genotype • treatment • interaction

The model should reflect how the experiment was actually performed — not just the comparison we hope to see.

Alignment Concepts

Why do we align sequencing reads?

Core idea

Sequencing gives us millions of short reads, but the reads themselves do not tell us where in the genome or transcriptome they originated.

Alignment answers the question:

“Where does each read best fit in the reference sequence?”

DNA alignment vs RNA alignment

DNA-seq alignment

Reads are aligned directly to the reference genome.

Typical applications:

  • Whole-genome sequencing
  • Whole-exome sequencing
  • Targeted panels
  • ChIP-seq
  • ATAC-seq

Common aligners:

BWA-MEM2, Bowtie2, DRAGEN

RNA-seq alignment

Reads often span exon–exon junctions.

Therefore, the aligner must be splice-aware.

Typical aligners:

STAR, HISAT2, DRAGEN RNA

Why RNA-seq needs a splice-aware aligner

A genomic read may align continuously:

Genome:
ACTGACCTGACCTGACCTGACCTG
        |||||||||||||
Read:   CCTGACCTGACCT

But an RNA-seq read can span two exons:

Exon 1                Exon 2
|---------|          |---------|
ACGTACGTAC          TGCATGCATG
      \________________/
          RNA read

The intron exists in the genome, but it has been removed from the mature RNA molecule.

What happens during RNA alignment?

A splice-aware aligner recognizes that one part of a read may map to one exon and the rest to another exon.

Example: exon–exon junction alignment

Reference genome

Exon 1        Intron                     Exon 2
==========-----------------------------==========

RNA-seq read

       =============================>
       part maps here      part maps here

In the BAM CIGAR string, skipped intronic sequence is often represented by N.

Example:

50M1000N50M

Meaning:

  • 50 bases aligned
  • 1000 genomic bases skipped as an intron
  • 50 more bases aligned

What reference files are needed?

Reference FASTA

Contains the actual nucleotide sequence of the genome.

Example:

GRCh38.fa

Used to determine:

Where can the read align?

Annotation GTF / GFF

Contains known gene and transcript structures.

Example information:

  • gene coordinates
  • exon coordinates
  • transcript IDs
  • gene IDs
  • strand

FASTA vs GTF: different roles

The FASTA tells the aligner what sequence exists.

The GTF tells the aligner where known genes, exons and splice junctions are located.

What information is inside a GTF?

A typical GTF line may look like:

chr1  HAVANA  exon  11869  12227  .  +  .  gene_id "ENSG...";

Important fields include:

  • chromosome
  • feature type: gene / transcript / exon
  • start and end coordinates
  • strand
  • gene ID
  • transcript ID

For gene-level counting, the annotation defines which aligned reads belong to which genes.

Why FASTA and GTF must match

A common analysis failure

Using a genome FASTA and annotation GTF from different builds or releases.

Examples:

FASTA: GRCh38
GTF:   GRCh37

or:

FASTA chromosome: chr1
GTF chromosome:   1

Reference FASTA, GTF/GFF, chromosome naming and gene IDs should be mutually compatible.

How an aligner works conceptually

The aligner evaluates:

  • matches
  • mismatches
  • insertions / deletions
  • splice junctions
  • multiple possible locations

Unique mapping vs multi-mapping

Unique mapping

A read has one clearly best genomic location.

This is generally easier to interpret.

Multi-mapping

A read maps equally well to several locations.

Common causes:

  • repetitive sequence
  • gene families
  • pseudogenes
  • paralogs

Mapping quality: how confident is the alignment?

MAPQ

Mapping quality reflects the confidence that a read has been placed at the correct genomic location.

Conceptually:

High MAPQ  → confident / unique alignment
Low MAPQ   → ambiguous placement

MAPQ is not the same as base quality.

  • Base quality = confidence in the nucleotide call
  • Mapping quality = confidence in the read’s genomic placement

What does an RNA aligner produce?

Typical outputs include:

  • aligned BAM
  • mapping statistics
  • splice-junction information
  • unmapped reads
  • alignment log files

What is stored in the BAM file?

Each aligned read can contain information such as:

  • read name
  • chromosome
  • genomic position
  • alignment flag
  • MAPQ
  • CIGAR string
  • mate information
  • sequence
  • quality values
  • optional tags

The BAM file is the bridge between raw sequencing reads and downstream genomic interpretation.

SAM vs BAM

SAM

Sequence Alignment/Map

A human-readable text representation of aligned reads.

Useful for understanding alignment fields and formats.

BAM

Binary compressed representation of SAM.

Smaller, faster to process and commonly used for downstream analysis.

SAM and BAM contain the same core alignment information; BAM is the practical format used for large sequencing datasets.

How to read one alignment conceptually

Read name     chromosome   position   MAPQ   CIGAR
READ001       chr1         105234     60     50M

This tells us:

  • which read was aligned;
  • where it aligned;
  • how confident the placement is;
  • how the read matches the reference.

For paired-end RNA-seq, additional fields describe the mate and fragment relationship.

What is the SAM flag?

The SAM flag is a compact numeric code describing alignment properties.

Examples of information encoded by flags include:

  • paired or single-end;
  • properly paired;
  • read mapped or unmapped;
  • mate mapped or unmapped;
  • forward or reverse strand;
  • first or second read in a pair;
  • secondary or supplementary alignment.

The flag does not represent one property; it combines multiple yes/no properties into one integer.

CIGAR string: how the read aligns

A CIGAR string summarizes how read bases relate to the reference.

Common operations:

Symbol Meaning
M aligned match/mismatch
I insertion relative to reference
D deletion relative to reference
N skipped reference region, often an intron
S soft clipping

Example:

50M1000N50M

means:

50 aligned bases → 1000 bp skipped intron → 50 aligned bases

Why BAM sorting and indexing matter

Coordinate sorting places reads in genomic order.

Indexing allows software to jump directly to a genomic region without scanning the whole file.

What does SAMtools do conceptually?

SAMtools is commonly used to work with alignment files.

Typical roles include:

  • converting between SAM and BAM;
  • sorting alignments;
  • indexing BAM files;

Alignment QC before counting

Before converting alignments into counts, ask:

  • What fraction of reads mapped?
  • How many mapped uniquely?
  • Are many reads multi-mapped?
  • Are paired-end reads behaving as expected?
  • Are splice junctions being detected?
  • Is there excessive rRNA or intergenic signal?
  • Are there obvious sample-to-sample abnormalities?

A count matrix can only be trusted if the underlying alignments are biologically and technically plausible.

From aligned reads to gene assignment

A read can overlap:

  • an exon from one gene;
  • multiple features;
  • no annotated feature;
  • an ambiguous region.

The counting software applies assignment rules to determine whether the read contributes to a gene count.

Counting is not simply “one mapped read = one gene”.

featureCounts concept

Important concepts include:

  • single-end vs paired-end data;

What happens to reads that cannot be assigned?

Some reads may remain unassigned because they are:

  • intergenic;
  • intronic;
  • multi-mapping;
  • overlapping multiple genes;
  • inconsistent with strandedness;
  • below quality filters.

The percentage of assigned reads is an important counting QC metric, not just the final gene-count table.

After alignment: how do reads become gene counts?

The counting step asks:

Does this aligned read overlap an annotated gene feature?

Why strandedness matters

Some RNA-seq libraries preserve information about which strand produced the RNA.

If strandedness is specified incorrectly during counting:

  • reads may be assigned to the wrong gene;
  • many valid reads may remain unassigned;
  • gene counts can be severely distorted.

Always determine whether the library is unstranded, forward-stranded or reverse-stranded.

Alignment is not the end of QC

Alignment QC tells us whether the sequencing reads behave as expected relative to the chosen reference.

Module 5 — Statistical Testing for RNA-seq

What are we testing for each gene?

For every gene, we ask whether the observed difference between conditions is larger than expected from biological and sampling variability.

Null hypothesis

There is no systematic expression difference between the groups.

Conceptually:

H₀: effect = 0

Alternative hypothesis

There is evidence of an expression difference between the groups.

Conceptually:

H₁: effect ≠ 0

RNA-seq differential expression is therefore a gene-by-gene hypothesis-testing problem, repeated across thousands of genes.

Effect size and statistical evidence are different

A differential-expression result usually contains both:

Effect size

Usually summarized as:

log2 fold change

How large is the expression difference?

Statistical evidence

Usually summarized by:

p-value / adjusted p-value

How strongly do the data argue against the null hypothesis?

A tiny fold change can be statistically significant, and a large fold change can be uncertain.

Interpret effect size and uncertainty together.

Why RNA-seq needs a count model

RNA-seq counts are:

  • non-negative integers;
  • affected by sequencing depth;
  • biologically variable;
  • often more variable than a simple Poisson model allows.

Both DESeq2 and edgeR model RNA-seq counts using the negative binomial distribution.

Mean and dispersion

The negative binomial model separates two important ideas:

Mean       → expected expression level
Dispersion → extra variability around that mean

Dispersion

Dispersion captures variability that is greater than expected from simple counting noise.

Genes with the same mean expression can have different levels of biological variability.

Reliable dispersion estimation is especially important when the number of biological replicates is limited.

Why borrow information across genes?

Estimating variability independently for every gene can be unstable.

Both major RNA-seq frameworks therefore use information across many genes to stabilize dispersion estimates.

The exact mathematical strategy differs between DESeq2 and edgeR, but both use cross-gene information to improve inference.

A p-value is not the probability that a gene is truly differential

The p-value asks:

If the null hypothesis were true, how surprising would data this extreme be?

It does not directly tell us:

  • the probability that H₀ is true;
  • the biological importance of the gene;
  • the size of the effect.

Why multiple-testing correction is essential

RNA-seq can test tens of thousands of genes simultaneously.

If we tested:

20,000 genes × p < 0.05

many apparently significant genes could occur by chance.

False Discovery Rate

The commonly reported adjusted p-value controls the false discovery rate (FDR).

A threshold such as:

padj < 0.05

means the analysis is controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries among the reported discoveries under the chosen procedure.

For RNA-seq, report adjusted p-values, not only raw p-values.

Statistical significance is not biological significance

A defensible interpretation considers:

Useful questions:

  • Is the effect large enough to matter biologically?
  • Is expression consistent across replicates?
  • Is the gene expressed at a meaningful level?
  • Does the result fit a coherent biological pattern?

DESeq2 and edgeR

Two major frameworks for count-based differential expression

DESeq2

Bioconductor package for negative-binomial generalized linear modeling.

Commonly used for:

  • bulk RNA-seq;
  • multifactor designs;
  • contrasts;
  • Wald tests;
  • likelihood-ratio tests.

edgeR

Bioconductor package for negative-binomial models with strong support for generalized linear models.

Commonly used for:

  • bulk RNA-seq;
  • multifactor designs;
  • exact tests;
  • GLM likelihood-ratio tests;
  • quasi-likelihood testing.

What DESeq2 and edgeR have in common

Both frameworks:

  • start from raw gene-level counts;
  • model counts using the negative binomial distribution;
  • account for library-size differences;
  • estimate gene-level dispersion;
  • support complex experimental designs;
  • perform gene-wise statistical tests;
  • correct for multiple testing.

They share the same broad statistical problem, but differ in how several steps are estimated and tested.

Normalization: DESeq2 vs edgeR

DESeq2

Common default:

median-of-ratios size factors

Goal:

estimate relative library scaling while reducing sensitivity to highly expressed genes.

edgeR

Common approach:

TMM normalization

Trimmed Mean of M-values estimates scaling factors while reducing the impact of strongly changing or highly abundant genes.

Neither method turns counts into TPM/FPKM for hypothesis testing.

They estimate scaling factors used inside the statistical model.

Dispersion estimation: conceptual difference

DESeq2

Estimates gene-wise dispersion and shrinks noisy estimates toward a learned mean-dispersion trend.

edgeR

Estimates common/trended/tagwise dispersion and uses empirical-Bayes strategies to stabilize gene-level variability.

The implementation differs, but the shared goal is the same:

avoid unreliable gene-by-gene variance estimates when replication is limited.

Testing strategies differ

DESeq2

Common choices:

  • Wald test — commonly used for individual coefficients / contrasts;
  • Likelihood-ratio test (LRT) — useful for comparing nested models.

edgeR

Common choices:

  • Exact test — simple two-group comparisons;
  • GLM likelihood-ratio test;
  • Quasi-likelihood F-test — widely used for robust GLM inference.

Wald test concept

The Wald test asks whether an estimated coefficient is sufficiently far from zero relative to its uncertainty.

Conceptually:

estimated effect
----------------
standard error

Large absolute values provide stronger evidence against the null hypothesis.

In DESeq2, this is the common default for standard differential-expression contrasts.

Likelihood-ratio test concept

The likelihood-ratio test compares:

Full model
vs
Reduced model

Example concept:

Full:    batch + condition
Reduced: batch

Question:

Does including condition significantly improve the model?

LRTs are particularly useful when testing the overall contribution of a factor or comparing nested models.

edgeR quasi-likelihood concept

edgeR’s quasi-likelihood framework adds an additional layer for modeling uncertainty in dispersion.

Conceptually:

This provides a conservative and flexible testing framework, especially for complex designs.

Fold-change shrinkage

Large fold changes from very low-count or noisy genes can be unstable.

DESeq2

Can apply log2 fold-change shrinkage after model fitting to improve effect-size estimation and ranking.

edgeR

Provides related approaches for testing biologically meaningful effect sizes, including threshold-based testing such as glmTreat.

Shrinkage or threshold testing changes how we interpret effect size, not simply whether a raw p-value is small.

DESeq2 vs edgeR — conceptual summary

Concept DESeq2 edgeR
Count model Negative binomial Negative binomial
Typical normalization Median-of-ratios TMM
Dispersion strategy Trend + shrinkage Empirical-Bayes common/trended/tagwise
Common simple test Wald Exact test / GLM
Complex designs GLM GLM
Model comparison LRT LRT
Robust GLM inference Wald/LRT framework Quasi-likelihood framework

Which should we use?

There is no universal rule that one package is always better.

Choose based on:

  • experimental design;
  • analysis conventions in your group;
  • desired testing framework;
  • reproducibility;
  • familiarity with diagnostics;
  • consistency across a project.

For this workshop, we will implement the practical R workflow using DESeq2, while understanding how edgeR addresses the same statistical problem using related but different strategies.

Module 5 — DESeq2 Fundamentals

Why not compare raw counts directly?

DESeq2 models counts using a negative binomial framework.

DESeq2 model

\[ K_{ij} \sim NB(\mu_{ij}, \alpha_i) \]

  • \(K_{ij}\) = observed count for gene \(i\) in sample \(j\)
  • \(\mu_{ij}\) = expected count
  • \(\alpha_i\) = gene-specific dispersion

DESeq2 workflow

Interpreting log2 fold change

Positive log2FC

Higher expression in Treated relative to Control.

Negative log2FC

Lower expression in Treated relative to Control.

Multiple-testing correction

A common workshop threshold:

padj < 0.05
AND
|log2FoldChange| >= 1

This is a reporting threshold, not a universal biological law.

Module 6 — Exploratory Analysis

Why transform counts for visualization?

Raw counts are appropriate for count-based statistical modeling, but their variance strongly depends on abundance.

Transformation is mainly used to make sample relationships and expression patterns easier to visualize.

It does not replace the raw-count model used by DESeq2.

PCA — what question does it answer?

Principal Component Analysis

PCA summarizes thousands of genes into a few major axes of variation.

Ask:

  • Do biological replicates cluster together?
  • Is condition a major source of variation?
  • Is there a batch effect?
  • Is one sample behaving unusually?

Sample-distance heatmap

A sample-distance heatmap asks:

How similar or dissimilar is every sample to every other sample?

Expected pattern:

biological replicates should usually be more similar to each other than to unrelated conditions.

Useful for detecting:

  • outliers;
  • sample swaps;
  • unexpected subgroups;
  • batch structure.

A volcano plot combines two dimensions:

X-axis

How large is the expression difference?

Y-axis

statistical evidence

A volcano plot is a useful summary, but interpretation should still consider sample-level expression patterns and biological context.

Heatmap — expression patterns across samples

Heatmaps are useful for visualizing coordinated expression patterns across a selected set of genes.

Look for:

GSEA

Module 14 — Interpretation Framework

A defensible interpretation sequence

Avoid common interpretation errors

  • Using p < 0.05 without multiple-testing correction.
  • Treating tiny significant fold changes as automatically important.
  • Using TPM / FPKM directly as DESeq2 input.
  • Ignoring PCA outliers.
  • Ignoring batch or paired structure.
  • Treating every GO term as an independent discovery.
  • Using an inappropriate ORA background.
  • Ignoring failed or duplicated ID mappings.

Final Take-home Messages

Five principles to remember

1. Experimental design determines what can be inferred.

2. QC is continuous — from FASTQ through biological interpretation.

3. Use raw counts for count-based differential-expression models.

4. Effect size, uncertainty and sample-level patterns must be interpreted together.

5. Enrichment summarizes gene-level results; it does not prove pathway activation.
